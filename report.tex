\section{Introduction}
In modern science to solve complex problems various simulations based on mathematical models are used. By doing so, researchers are able to quickly test hypotheses, change parameters and solve equations that are too complex or impossible to physically recreate. One of these mathematical tools is Finite Element Method (FEM) that is used to solve differntial equations.

Many physical systems are governed by partial differential equations whose analytical solutions are either extremely difficult or impossible to obtain, that is the reason why numerical analysis comes in. It allows to design and implememt an algorithms to find approximate but highly accurate numeric solutions. 

So, to solve the elliptic partial differential problems we will use the numerical method which finds the solutions accurately, however still has the error, so in this project the finite element approximation will be explored and will be checked how accurately it gives the solution and how quickly the solution goes to the desirable value (convergence analysis) by comparing with the known previously obtained results.
In this project 2 important eigenproblems will be explored: Laplace and the Time-Independent \text{Schr\"{o}dinger}.



\section{Mathematical Background}


\subsection{Elliptic partial differential equations}
One of the branches of differenial equations is elliptic differential equation, which has the general form:
\begin{equation}
A*u_{xx} + B*u_{xy} + C*u_{yy} + D*u_{x} + E*u_{y} + F*u = G (1)
\end{equation}

The equation (1) is classified as elliptic if the discriminant is strictly negative:
\begin{equation}
B^2 - 4*A*C < 0
\end{equation}

Elliptic equations differ from hyperbolic and parabolic equations because they describe the steady state, time-independent phenomena. Moreover, their solutions depend only on spatial variables and require boundary conditions rather than initial conditions, the most common are Dirichlet (specifying the value on the boundary) and Neumann (specifying the derivative or the flux at the boundary). In addition to this, changes at any point of the domain influences the solution, reflecting the global nature of elliptic problems.


\subsection{Eigenvalue problems}
Mathematically, an eigenvalue problem is an equation in which a linear transformation applied to a specific vector - eigenvector, results in the exact same vector scaled by a constant multiplier - eigenvalue. The basic eigenvalue problem for a linear transformation A acting on a nonzero vector x is defined as:
\begin{equation}
A\vec{x} = \lambda\vec{x}
\end{equation}
where $A$ is an $n \times n$ matrix or linear operator which is known; $x$ is an eigenvector - a nonzero column vector that represents the main direction of the system, which need to be found; $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue - a scalar that needs to be found.
The main idea is that when the transformation A operates on x, the eigenvector does not change its direction, it only stretches, compresses, or flips.


\subsection{Generalised matrix eigenvalue problem}
Another variation of the eigenvalue problem is the $generalised eigenvalue problem$. It can be represented in two forms:

- the vector form:
\begin{equation}
A\vec{x_{i}} = \lambda_{i} * B * \vec{x_{i}}
\end{equation}
- the matrix form:
\begin{equation}
AX = BXD
\end{equation}
Where $X$ is the column matrix of the eigenvectors; $D$ is a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues; $B$ acts as a weighting matrix or a matrix tensor that defines a new inner product for the vector space. 

In other words, it changes the geometry of the problem by measuring vectors not in the standard Euclidean space, but relative to the properties of $B$ itself [1]. It usually represents a specific geometric or physical constraint [2]. 

In this project the generalised eigenvalue problem was chosen because it is better when comparing systems with dual constraints (physical forces and their underlying mass). In addition, because both (in the Laplace case) matrices are symmetric, the solvers preserve these structural properties, guaranteeing real eigenvalues. Also, solving generalised problem avoids explicitly forming $B^{-1}A$, preserving symmetry and improving numerical accuracy, which would be useful for both Laplace and \text{Schr\"{o}dinger} problems.


\subsection{Weak formulation}
Weak formulation is an approach of multiplying the differential equation by an arbitrary test function that will disappear at the end. More precisely it is an integral representation that relaxes the strict smoothness required for the $strong form$, therefore it requires the equation to be satisfied in an average or integral sense [4].

The way from the strong form to weak requires following steps:

Firstly, write the strong form (we will take Poisson equation on the interval I with Dirichlet boundary condition as an example):
\begin{equation}
    \begin{cases}
        -u''(x) = f(x), where x \in I = [0, L] \\
        u(0) = u(L) = 0
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Then, multiply by a test function (rewrite as variational formulation):
\begin{equation}
-u''(x) * v(x) = f(x) * v(x)
\end{equation}

Where $v(x)$ is a continuous test function which is assumed to vanish at end points.

Finally, integrate over the domain using integration by parts (in our case the domain is the interval I = [0, L]):
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}     
    \int_{0}^{L}f(x) \cdot v(x) \, dx 
        &= \int_{0}^{L}-u''(x) \cdot v(x) \, dx \\ 
        &= \left[ -v(x) \cdot u'(x) \right]\Big|_{0}^{L} \\ 
        &\quad + \int_{0}^{L}u'(x) \cdot v'(x) \, dx \\ 
        &= \int_{0}^{L}u'(x) \cdot v'(x) \, dx \\ 
        &\quad - u'(L) \cdot v(L) + u'(0) \cdot v(0) \\
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Since $v(0) = v(L) = 0$ by Dirichlet BCs:
\begin{equation}
    \int_{0}^{L}f(x) \cdot v(x) \, dx = \int_{0}^{L}u'(x) \cdot v'(x) \, dx
\end{equation}

The form obtained in (9) is called a weak form in 1D. For 2D and higher dimentions integration by parts follows the divergence theorem is used, in particular the Green's Theorem presented in (10):
\begin{equation}
    \int_{\Omega}-\Delta{u}v \, dx = \int_{\Omega}\nabla{u} \dot \nabla{v} \, dx - \int_{\partial{\Omega}} n \dot \nabla{u}v \, ds 
\end{equation}

We are going from strong form to the weak one because weak form allows us to solve problems where derivatives might be discontinuous or non-existent and it handles the boundary conditions naturally. In addition to this, it reduces the order of derivatives, which can simplify further computations.


\subsection{Bilinear forms}
In partial differential equations, a bilinear form is a mapping function $B: V \times V \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ that takes two functions from a vector space and returns a scalar. In the weak formulation of a PDE, it represents the differential operator and boundary conditions. It is also linear in both arguments independently [6]. This is illustrated in (11).

Linearity in the first argument:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    B(u + \alpha v, w) = B(u, w) + \alpha B(v, w) \\
    \text{Linearity in the second argument:} \\
    B(u, v + \alpha w) = B(u, v) + \alpha B(u, w)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Therefore, the left hand side of weak formulation of the Poisson equation (9) can be transformed into the abstract variational form - find $u \in V$ such that it will satisfy the (12).
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    B(u, v) &= L(v) \, \forall v \in V, where \\
    B(u, v) &= \int_{\Omega}\nabla{u} \dot \nabla{v} \, dx \\
    L(v) &= \int_{\Omega}fv \, dx
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}


\subsection{Sobolev and Hilbert spaces}
The main challenge for the numerical analysis is accepting that real world problems, discrete data sets and constraints fundamentally lack smoothness. Of course it is possible to force artificial smoothness, but it will lead to inefficiency, information loss or incorrect interpretation. That is why, in numerical methods scientists do not use functions from $C^{n}(\Omega)$ space, becuase requiring continuous derivatives is too restrictive for approximate solutions. That is where the Sobolev and Hilbert spaces come in.

Finite Element Method (FEM) uses piecewise-defined basis functions (this topic would be discussed later). Within each element the function might be perfectly smooth, but across element boundaries, the derivative is discontinuous. Therefore, we need to use the space that will allow us to have discontinuous derivatives, which is Sobolev space, however to be able to introduce the Sobolev space fully we need first to introduce the Lebesque function space. 

Lebesque function space is a space of "p-th power integrable" functions which are defined by (13). 
\begin{equation}
    f \in L^p if \int |f|^p < \infty 
\end{equation}
And their norms or lengths are defined in (14):
\begin{equation}
    ||f||_{L^p} = \Bigg(\int_{\Omega} |f|^p \, dx \Bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}
\end{equation}

When $p=2$, $L^{2}(\Omega)$ space possesses an inner product, allowing us to define angles, distances and perform orthogonal projections.

The Sobolev space, which is typically denoted as $W^{k, p}(\Omega)$, consists of all functions in $L^{p}(\Omega)$ whose weak (or distributional) derivatives up to order $k$ also belong to $L^{p}(\Omega)$. We are interested in Sobolev spaces because of its special case $H^{k}(\Omega)$ also known as Hilbert space, which uses the $L^2$ norm. For solving PDEs we are going to use $H^{1}(\Omega)$ since it only requires first order derivatives which we have according to (9). Since we know that we have the Dirichlet boundary condition we can introduce $H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)$ that represents functions in the space $H^{1}(\Omega)$ whose boundary values are zero.

But why did we need all these spaces? The answer is that for the FEM if our functions will belong to the $H^1$ it requires the derivatives to be square integrable, but not continuous, therefore we will be able to integrate (9) over our domain full of piecewise polynomials. 


\subsection{Existence of the solution}
Two of the most important questions in mathematics are the questions of existence and uniqueness. So what we want to ask while solving the PDE using FEM is why do we have a solution and is this solution unique. The answer for these questions is the Lax-Miligram theorem.

According to Lax-Miligram theorem: for every bounded linear functional $f$ on $H$, there exists a unique $x_{f} \in H$ such that (15) is true. 
\begin{equation}
f(x) = \phi(x, x_{f}) \forall x \in H
\end{equation}

Where $\phi(x, x_f)$ is a bounded coercive bilinear form of a Hilbert space [7].

It is worth mentioning that Lax-Miligram theorem follows as a corollary to the Stampacchia theorems that states: any function $f \in H$ necessearily corresponds to a unique function $u \in H$ for which the inequality (16) is satisfied for all functions $v \in H$.
\begin{equation}
    \phi(u, v - u) \geq \langle f, v - u \rangle_H
\end{equation}
Where $\langle f, v - u \rangle_H$ is an inner product, which in Hilbert spaces defined by (17).
\begin{equation}
    \langle u, v \rangle_H^{1}(\Omega) = \int_{\Omega}(uv + \nabla u \dot \nabla v) \, dx
\end{equation}

So using (12) we will have that $a(u, v)$ is a bilinear form and $L(v)$ is a linear functional. 

Firstly, we need to say that $a(u, v)$ is bounded, in other words $|a(u, v)| \leq M{||u||}||v||$ (the proof can be found in [8]).

Then we show that our bilinear form is coercive (or elliptical) which is $a(v, v) \geq \alpha ||v||_{H^1}^2$ for some $\alpha > 0$ (the proof can be found in Appendix A).

To establish continuity of $L(v)$, we must show that $|L(v)| \leq C_{L}||v||_{H^1}$ for a constant $C_{L} > 0$ (proof can be found in Appendix A).

Therefore, by the Lax-Milgram Theorem these properties guaratee that a unique exact solution $u \in V$ exists and stable.


\subsection{The Rayleigh-Ritz/Galerkin approximation}
The idea explored in this section is the core of the Finite Element Approximation (FEA). The solution of PDEs belongs to infinite-dimensional space, so our goal is to go from the infinite-dimentional space to finite-dimentional subspace. To do so we cn use one of two methods: Rayleigh-Ritz approximation or Galerkin approximation which will give the equivalent solution of the variational problem [9].

So, we will go through the Rayleigh-Ritz approximation process. We will look at the arbitrary problem $\lambda w = -\Delta w$, whose weak form can be expressed as in (18).
\begin{equation}
    \lambda \int_{\Omega}wv \, dx = - \int_{\Omega}\nabla{w} \dot \nabla{v} \, dx
\end{equation}

By definition of the weak form, the equation must hold for any arbitrary function $v$, therefore if it holds for every basis function defined by $\phi_j$ that spans the subspace, then it holds for any linear combination of them. So, we can rewrite (18) as it is shown in (19).
\begin{equation}
     \lambda \int_{\Omega}w\phi_j \, dx = - \int_{\Omega}\nabla{w} \dot \nabla{\phi_j} \, dx
\end{equation}

Recalling the definition of the inner product $\langle u, v \rangle = \int_{a}^{b}uv \, dx$ helps us to turn (19) into (20).
\begin{equation}
    \lambda \langle w, \phi_j \rangle = \langle \nabla{w} \dot \nabla{\phi_j} \rangle
\end{equation}

Let $w_{1}, \dots , w_{n}$ be $n$ arbitrary trial functions. Therefore, because we project our infinite-dimentional problem onto finite-dimensional subspace we can express our unknown function $w$ as the linear combination of our trial functions which is shown in (21).
\begin{equation}
    w = \sum^n_{k=1}c_k w_k
\end{equation}

Substituting (21) into the (20) gives us (22).
\begin{equation}
    \lambda \langle \sum^n_{k=1}c_k w_k, \phi_j \rangle = \langle \nabla \Bigg(\sum^n_{k=1}c_k w_k\Bigg) \dot \nabla{\phi_j} \rangle
\end{equation}

Because our $w$ is a closed subspace, it inherits the same boundness and coercivity constants, so if we reapply Max-Milgram theorem, we will prove that the discrete FEM has a unique solution.

Now we know that solution exists and is unique, but how do we find it?

Following the properties of the inner product we can rewrite (22) as (23).
\begin{equation}
    \lambda \sum^n_{k=1}c_k\langle w_k, \phi_j \rangle = \sum^n_{k=1}c_k \langle \nabla{w_k} \dot \nabla{\phi_j} \rangle
\end{equation}

This allows us to define (24).
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    A = (a_jk) &= \langle w_k, \phi_j \rangle \\
    M = &= \langle \nabla{w_k} \dot \nabla{\phi_j} \rangle \\
    \vec{c} &= (c_k)^T
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

So, (24) transforms into (25).
\begin{equation}
    \lambda M\vec{c} = A\vec{c} 
\end{equation}
Which is exactly the form of the generalised eigenvalue problem, where $A$ is so-called stiffness matrix and $M$ is a mass matrix (the properties and meaning of those matrices will be cleared later). 

This whole process is called finite element discretisation. This process is what makes FEA so powerful because it allows us to breake down a complex continuous physical domain into a finite number of smaller, interconnected subregions (elements). This transforms complex governing differential equations into a manageable system of algebraic equations that can be solved numerically.

The next chapter will describe what are the basis functions that we use to break the problem from infinite-dimentions to finite, how they are defined and how to use them to solve elliptic PDEs.

\section{Proof of coercivity}

To prove that \(a(v,v) \ge \alpha \Vert{}v\Vert{}_{H^1}^2\) for some \(\alpha > 0\), evaluate the bilinear form at identical arguments:

\(a(v,v)=\int _{\Omega }\nabla v\cdot \nabla v\,dx=\int _{\Omega }|\nabla v|{}^{2}\,dx=\|\nabla v\|{}_{L^{2}}^{2}\)

Because \(\Omega \) is a bounded domain, the Poincaré inequality asserts that there exists a positive constant \(C_{\Omega }\) depending only on the geometry of \(\Omega \) such that:

\(\|v\|{}_{L^{2}}^{2}\le C_{\Omega }^{2}\|\nabla v\|{}_{L^{2}}^{2}\quad \forall v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega )\)

Relate this inequality to the full \(H^{1}\)-norm:

\(\|v\|{}_{H^{1}}^{2}=\|v\|{}_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla v\|{}_{L^{2}}^{2}\le C_{\Omega }^{2}\|\nabla v\|{}_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla v\|{}_{L^{2}}^{2}=(C_{\Omega }^{2}+1)\|\nabla v\|{}_{L^{2}}^{2}\)

Isolating the gradient term gives:
\(\|\nabla v\|{}_{L^{2}}^{2}\ge \frac{1}{C_{\Omega }^{2}+1}\|v\|{}_{H^{1}}^{2}\)

Substituting this back into the expression for \(a(v,v)\) completes the proof of coercivity:
\(a(v,v)\ge \alpha \|v\|{}_{H^{1}}^{2}\quad \text{where\ }\alpha =\frac{1}{C_{\Omega }^{2}+1}>0\)

\chapter{Proof of Bound Linear Functional}

To establish the continuity of \(L(v)\), we must show that \(\vert{}L(v)\vert{} \le C_L \Vert{}v\Vert{}_{H^1}\) for a constant \(C_L > 0\). Apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in \(L^2(\Omega)\) to the definition of the linear functional:

\(|L(v)|=\left|\int _{\Omega }fv\,dx\right|\le \|f\|{}_{L^{2}}\|v\|{}_{L^{2}}\)

By the definition of the \(H^{1}\)-norm, it is clear that \(\Vert{}v\Vert{}_{L^2} \le \Vert{}v\Vert{}_{H^1}\) because \(\Vert{}v\Vert{}_{H^1} = \sqrt{\Vert{}v\Vert{}_{L^2}^2 + \Vert{}\nabla v\Vert{}_{L^2}^2}\). Substituting this property into the upper bound gives:
\(|L(v)|\le \|f\|{}_{L^{2}}\|v\|{}_{H^{1}}\)

Setting \(C_L = \Vert{}f\Vert{}_{L^2}\) satisfies the continuity condition:
\(|L(v)|\le C_{L}\|v\|{}_{H^{1}}\)

Since \(f \in L^2(\Omega)\), the value \(C_{L}\) is a finite constant independent of the choice of \(v\), confirming that \(L\) is a bounded, continuous linear operator on \(H^1_0(\Omega)\).
